Module 1: What Are Succulents?

Specialist foundation • ≈ 20–25 minutes

1. Botanical definition and key adaptations

Succulents are plants that have evolved specialised water-storage tissues in leaves, stems or roots. This allows them to survive extended periods of drought. The defining feature is the presence of water-storage parenchyma, often accompanied by thick cuticles, reduced stomata, CAM (Crassulacean Acid Metabolism) photosynthesis in many genera, and morphological features that reduce surface-area-to-volume ratio.

True cacti (family Cactaceae) are a subset of succulents. Most other popular “succulents” belong to families such as Crassulaceae (Echeveria, Sedum, Crassula, Graptopetalum), Asphodelaceae (Aloe, Haworthia, Gasteria), and Aizoaceae. Understanding the family helps predict care needs.

2. Why these adaptations matter for cultivation

  • Water storage means the plant can tolerate (and often prefers) drying cycles. Constant moisture leads to root hypoxia and rot.
  • CAM photosynthesis (common in many genera) means stomata open mainly at night. This reduces daytime water loss but also means growth responds strongly to night-time temperatures and humidity.
  • Shallow or specialised root systems in many species make them sensitive to poor drainage and compacted media.
  • Light requirements are generally high because many originated in open, high-irradiance habitats. Insufficient light produces etiolation (elongated, weak growth) and loss of compact form and colour.

3. Major groups you will encounter

  • Echeveria & related Crassulaceae – Rosette formers, often colourful under good light, prone to etiolation indoors in the UK, sensitive to prolonged wet.
  • Haworthia & Gasteria – More shade-tolerant, slower growing, good for lower-light UK windowsills, still need free drainage.
  • Crassula (including Jade plant) – Variable; many are forgiving but still rot if kept wet in winter.
  • Aloe – Generally robust; some species tolerate cooler conditions better than Echeveria.
  • True cacti – Often need even stricter dry periods and higher light than leaf succulents.

4. Relevance to mental wellbeing and learning

The clear cause-and-effect relationship in succulent culture (light → form and colour; water → firmness or collapse; drainage → survival) makes them excellent subjects for observational learning. Small, visible responses to care adjustments provide frequent feedback, which many adults find intrinsically rewarding and calming.

Specialist deep dive: Botanical diagnosis and adaptation

Work through these practical investigations and compare your findings with the lesson above. Conditions and plant species matter more than universal rules.

1. Read the plant before prescribing care

Identify genus or species when possible. Record whether leaves, stems or roots store water, and whether the plant is actively growing. Do not assume every succulent follows the same seasonal schedule.

2. Water economy and CAM

Many succulents use crassulacean acid metabolism, opening stomata mainly at night to limit water loss. CAM varies between species and with conditions; it is not a universal rule for all succulents.

3. Field exercise

Compare a rosette Echeveria, an Aloe and a cactus. Sketch their water-storage organs, leaf or spine arrangements, and likely adaptations to their habitats.

4. Diagnostic reasoning

Separate observations from causes. Yellow leaves can reflect normal ageing, excess moisture, drought, light change or root damage. Check leaf firmness, root condition and growing medium before acting.
