Module 5: Propagation – Making New Plants

Techniques, physiology and aftercare • ≈ 25–30 minutes

1. Why propagation is central to specialist practice

Propagation allows multiplication of successful plants, recovery of plants that have become etiolated or damaged, and experimentation with different clones. Leaf and stem cuttings exploit the plant’s ability to form adventitious roots and shoots from differentiated tissue. Success rates vary by genus, season, and aftercare.

2. Leaf cuttings (Crassulaceae and some others)

Works reliably with many Echeveria, Graptopetalum, Pachyphytum, Sedum and some Crassula. Less reliable or different technique required for Haworthia, Aloe and most true cacti.

  1. Select a healthy, mature leaf. Twist or cut so that the entire leaf base is removed cleanly; incomplete bases reduce success.
  2. Allow the leaf to callus in dry, bright, indirect light for 2–7 days (longer in humid conditions). Callusing reduces infection risk when the wound contacts substrate.
  3. Place the leaf on (or very lightly insert the base into) dry or barely moist free-draining substrate. Do not bury deeply.
  4. Provide bright light without harsh midday scorch. Avoid frequent misting or watering; excess moisture encourages rot before roots form.
  5. Roots typically appear first, followed by a tiny plantlet at the leaf base. Timeframe is often 2–8 weeks depending on species and conditions.
  6. Once the plantlet has several leaves and a functional root system, it can be potted independently. The original leaf may wither and can be removed.

3. Stem cuttings

  1. Cut a healthy stem section with clean tools. Remove lower leaves to create a clear stem section for insertion.
  2. Callus the cut surface thoroughly (several days to a week or more for thicker stems).
  3. Insert into dry or barely moist free-draining medium. Support if necessary.
  4. Withhold significant water until new growth or resistance to gentle tug indicates rooting has begun.

Stem cuttings are often faster and more reliable than leaf cuttings for many species, and are the primary method for plants that do not leaf-propagate readily.

4. Offsets (pups)

Many Aloes, Haworthias, Agaves and some other genera produce lateral offsets. These can be separated once they have developed their own roots or are large enough to support independent growth. Allow any cut surfaces to callus before potting.

5. Common failure points and how to avoid them

  • Insufficient callusing → infection and rot.
  • Overly wet medium during the rooting phase → rot.
  • Very low light → weak or failed plantlets.
  • Impatience and early heavy watering.
  • Using leaves that were already stressed or incomplete.
