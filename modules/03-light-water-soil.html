Module 3: Light, Water & Soil

Core cultural principles • ≈ 25–30 minutes

1. Light – quantity, quality and plant response

Most popular succulents evolved under high irradiance. Indoors in the UK, light is the primary limiting factor for compact growth and pigmentation.

  • Intensity and duration: Aim for the brightest available position. South- or west-facing windowsills provide the highest useful light. East is often acceptable for more shade-tolerant genera (Haworthia, Gasteria). North is marginal for most Echeveria-type plants.
  • Etiolation: Stretching, increased internode length, and paling of colour indicate insufficient light. The plant is prioritising height over compactness. Correct by moving to brighter light; existing elongated growth will not reverse.
  • Colouration: Many Echeveria and related plants develop stronger reds, oranges and pinks under higher light and mild stress (cooler nights, controlled drought). This is anthocyanin and carotenoid expression, not damage when moderate.
  • Seasonal change: UK winter light levels drop dramatically. Growth slows or stops for many species; do not expect summer performance year-round.

2. Water – the critical variable

The single most important cultural rule: water thoroughly, then allow the substrate to dry substantially before watering again.

  • Thorough watering ensures the entire root ball is moistened and helps flush any accumulated salts.
  • Drying cycle restores oxygen to the root zone and prevents the anaerobic conditions that favour root-rot pathogens (Pythium, Phytophthora, Fusarium, etc.).
  • Frequency is not fixed by calendar. It depends on temperature, light, pot material, substrate porosity, plant size and season. In active growth (spring–summer) this may be every 7–21 days; in cool, low-light winter it may be every 4–8 weeks or longer.
  • Diagnostic cues: Firm, plump leaves usually indicate adequate water status. Wrinkling or deflation suggests the plant has drawn on stored water and may need irrigation. Soft, translucent or blackening tissue near the base usually indicates excess moisture and possible rot.

When in doubt, wait. Underwatering is almost always recoverable; advanced root rot often is not.

3. Substrate – physical properties over brand names

Roots of most succulents require high oxygen availability. The key physical properties are:

  • High air-filled porosity after drainage
  • Rapid drainage (water should flow through freely)
  • Low to moderate water-holding capacity

A practical mix: quality peat-free multipurpose or specialist cactus compost amended with 30–50% (by volume) inorganic material such as horticultural grit, perlite, pumice or coarse sand. Avoid fine sand that can compact. Never use unamended garden soil or moisture-retentive houseplant composts designed for tropical foliage plants.
