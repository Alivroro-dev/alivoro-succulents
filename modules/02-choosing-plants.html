Module 2: Choosing Plants & Tools

Specialist selection criteria • ≈ 20 minutes

1. Matching plant to environment

Success begins with selecting species whose natural requirements can realistically be met in your space. In the UK, most indoor growers face limited winter light and cool night temperatures. Prioritise plants that tolerate or prefer these conditions rather than forcing high-light, warmth-loving species into suboptimal sites.

  • Higher light / brighter windowsills (south or west): Echeveria, many Sedum, Graptopetalum, most columnar cacti, Aeonium (with care).
  • Moderate to lower light (east, north-east, bright north): Haworthia, Gasteria, some Aloe, Sansevieria/Dracaena (though not true succulents in the strict sense), certain Crassula.
  • Cooler winter tolerance: Many Haworthia, some Aloe, hardy Sedum (if outdoor), certain Opuntia.

2. Assessing plant health at purchase

  • Turgor and firmness: Leaves and stems should feel firm. Softness, translucency or collapse usually indicates overwatering or early rot.
  • Root and collar health: Avoid plants sitting in saturated media. Lift gently if possible; look for firm white/cream roots rather than brown, mushy or foul-smelling tissue.
  • Pests: Inspect leaf axils, undersides and the soil surface for mealybugs (white cottony masses), scale, or fungus gnat activity.
  • Etiolation: Elongated internodes and pale colour indicate prior low light. The plant can recover form with better light, but the stretched growth will remain.
  • Recent stress: Avoid plants that have been heavily discounted after obvious decline; recovery is possible but requires more skill.

3. Essential materials (quality over quantity)

  • Pots: Must have drainage holes. Terracotta provides better evaporation and root aeration; plastic retains moisture longer (useful only if you water sparingly).
  • Substrate: Commercial peat-free cactus/succulent compost is a starting point. For better results, amend with additional grit, perlite, pumice or coarse sand to increase air-filled porosity. Aim for rapid drainage and oxygen availability at the root zone.
  • Top dressing: Optional inorganic grit or fine gravel reduces surface moisture and splash, and improves appearance.
  • Tools: Clean sharp scissors or secateurs for cuttings; a small soft brush for cleaning; isopropyl alcohol for pest treatment.

4. Starting strategy for adults learning the craft

Begin with 2–4 resilient, clearly labelled plants from different groups (e.g. one Haworthia, one Echeveria, one Crassula). This allows comparative observation of how different genera respond to the same environment. Avoid rare or expensive specimens until basic cultural competence is established.
