Module 4: Pots & Planting

Root health and container culture • ≈ 20–25 minutes

1. Pot selection principles

  • Drainage holes are non-negotiable. Without free drainage, even a perfect substrate will eventually become waterlogged at the base.
  • Size: Choose a pot only modestly larger than the current root system. Oversized pots hold excess moisture in the unused medium and increase rot risk.
  • Material: Terracotta (unglazed) is generally preferable for beginners and for plants prone to overwatering because it allows evaporative loss through the walls and promotes faster drying. Plastic is lighter and retains moisture longer — usable if watering discipline is strict.
  • Shape: Shallower bowls suit many rosette succulents with fibrous roots; deeper pots suit plants with more extensive root systems. Avoid decorative containers without drainage unless used as cachepots (outer covers) with an inner drained pot.

2. Repotting procedure (best practice)

  1. Water the plant lightly a day or two beforehand only if the root ball is extremely dry and brittle; otherwise work with dryish medium for cleaner handling.
  2. Remove the plant carefully. Support the base of the leaves or stem. Do not pull by the foliage.
  3. Examine the roots. Healthy roots are firm and usually pale. Cut away any soft, black, brown or foul-smelling roots with clean tools. If significant rot is present, allow the remaining tissue to callus in dry air for several days before repotting.
  4. Remove as much of the old, degraded medium as practical, especially if it is compacted or stays wet.
  5. Place a small amount of fresh, free-draining substrate in the new pot. Position the plant so that the root crown / base of the leaves sits at or slightly above the final soil line.
  6. Backfill, firming gently to eliminate large air pockets without compacting heavily.
  7. Optional inorganic top dressing (grit) helps keep the neck dry and reduces fungus-gnat habitat.
  8. Critical aftercare: Do not water immediately. Allow 3–7 days (longer if roots were damaged or cut) so any wounds can suberise. Then water thoroughly and resume normal dry-down cycles.

3. Timing and frequency

Spring (as growth resumes) is the ideal window for routine repotting. Autumn and winter repotting is possible but recovery is slower and rot risk higher if conditions are cool and light is low. Most plants benefit from refreshing the medium every 1–2 years, or when roots have filled the pot, the substrate has broken down, or persistent moisture problems occur.

Specialist deep dive: Potting and root establishment

Work through these practical investigations and compare your findings with the lesson above. Conditions and plant species matter more than universal rules.

1. Choose containers by root system

Drainage holes are essential for straightforward cultivation. Choose pot size around the existing roots rather than an oversized reservoir of wet substrate.

2. Repot safely

Unpot gently, remove clearly rotten tissue with clean tools, and allow damaged surfaces to dry as appropriate for the plant. Do not remove healthy roots unnecessarily.

3. Aftercare

Keep a freshly repotted plant in bright, indirect light while it settles. Timing of first watering depends on root damage, season and species; avoid a rigid rule.

4. Failure analysis

If a plant declines after repotting, examine temperature, root loss, moisture and abrupt light changes before adding fertiliser.
