Module 6: Common Problems & Diagnostics

Systematic diagnosis and recovery • ≈ 25 minutes

1. Diagnostic framework

Work from symptoms to likely causes to actions. Ask: Is the tissue soft or firm? Is the problem localised or systemic? Recent cultural changes? Season and light levels? Always consider moisture history first — overwatering remains the dominant cause of serious decline in cultivation.

2. Soft, mushy or blackening tissue (usually excess moisture / rot)

  • Symptoms: Soft leaves or stem, translucent patches, black or brown progressive lesions, often starting at the base or soil line, possible foul odour.
  • Action: Remove from pot immediately. Cut away all soft and discoloured tissue back to clean, firm growth with sterile tools. Allow remaining material to dry and callus thoroughly in air. Repot into fresh, dry, highly free-draining medium only when callused. Withhold water for an extended period. In severe cases the plant may not be recoverable; healthy leaves can still be used for propagation.

3. Wrinkled, deflated or thinning leaves (usually underwatering or root loss)

The plant is drawing on stored water. If roots are intact, a thorough watering followed by normal dry-down will restore turgor within days. If roots have rotted and the plant cannot take up water, the same symptoms appear — this is why root inspection is essential when recovery does not follow watering.

4. Etiolation (stretching)

Insufficient light intensity or duration. Internodes lengthen, leaves may be smaller and paler, compact rosette form is lost. Correction requires increased light. Stretched growth is permanent; many growers later behead or take cuttings from the upper portion and re-root to restore a compact plant.

5. Pests

  • Mealybugs: White, cottony masses in axils and crevices. Treat locally with isopropyl alcohol on a cotton bud, or with appropriate contact treatments. Repeat monitoring is required; eggs and residual individuals often persist.
  • Fungus gnats: Indicate persistently moist surface or organic-rich medium. Allow more thorough drying; consider inorganic top dressing and reduced watering frequency.
  • Scale and aphids: Less common but possible; physical removal and targeted treatment.

6. Other physiological issues

  • Sun scorch: Sudden exposure to intense light after low-light conditions can bleach or burn tissue. Acclimatise gradually.
  • Cold damage: Soft, water-soaked patches after exposure to near-freezing temperatures. Prevent by keeping plants away from cold glass on freezing nights.
  • Nutrient issues: Generally secondary. Most succulents are not heavy feeders; excess fertiliser can do more harm than deficiency in typical indoor culture.

Specialist deep dive: Evidence-led plant diagnostics

Work through these practical investigations and compare your findings with the lesson above. Conditions and plant species matter more than universal rules.

1. Triage symptoms

Start with location of damage, speed of change and leaf texture. Soft translucent tissue suggests a different process from dry wrinkled tissue; neither alone proves a diagnosis.

2. Inspect roots and crown

Check for foul smell, black mushy tissue, pests and blocked drainage. If rot is suspected, isolate the plant and remove compromised tissue with clean tools.

3. Integrated pest management

Identify the pest before treating. Physically remove small infestations where possible, isolate affected plants and follow product labels and UK rules for any treatment.

4. Recovery monitoring

Photograph the same angle weekly, record whether new growth is healthy, and avoid repeated watering or fertilising as an automatic rescue response.
